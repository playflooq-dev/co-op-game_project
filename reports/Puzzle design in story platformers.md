# Make Every Puzzle Need Both Siblings

**Status (2026-11-25): historical, still useful.** Research from 2026-11-19 for the first city level. The game has
changed since (jumping, pictures instead of words, a younger audience), but the main lesson, that every puzzle
should need both siblings, is still what the levels are built on. What is decided now is in
[DECISIONS.md](../DECISIONS.md).

The story puzzle games that work best do three things together. First, the next thing the story needs *is* the puzzle: the player steals the submarine instead of being handed it. Second, each character has an action the other lacks, and those actions are chained so that one sibling's move opens the way for the other. Third, each new idea is taught alone, where failure costs nothing, and is then used together, twisted and retired before it gets stale. The games that disappoint fail in predictable ways. Reviewers called the co-op "you do your thing, I'll do mine" when each player just cleared their own obstacle. Other failures were switches and crates that belong to no real place, instant-death trial and error, long empty walks, a hard spike at the end, and one player waiting while the other works. For this game, that points to a clear plan. Keep the sister's reach and the brother's small size as fixed parts of who they are. Let each chapter add one new world system (water and sunlight in the forest) instead of new powers. Leave out free jumping and add a context "boost". Build nearly every puzzle from on/off or step-by-step states, which stay in sync easily online. Pair the ability split with an information split running the other way, so neither child is ever only the reader or only the doer. A 15-20 minute chapter can hold three or four teach-use-twist cycles, one rest beat, one short moment where the siblings are apart, a forgiving set piece and a map-piece reward. Built-in pings and picture stickers, soft failure, tiered hints kept in the map's margins, and code values that change each game (while the logic stays fixed) cover the rest. The forest should get harder by combining what the players already know, such as water level plus sunlight or height plus darkness, not by demanding fast timing.

## The best story games make the next story action the puzzle

This report draws on notes covering about forty games. They fall into three groups. The first is single-player story adventures (Inside, Limbo, Lost in Play, Planet of Lana I and II, Creaks, Machinarium, The Gardens Between, Bramble). The second is two-character and co-op platformers (Brothers, It Takes Two, Split Fiction, Never Alone, Unravel Two, Little Nightmares III, The Lost Vikings, Trine). The third is "talk to each other" games where each player sees different things (BOKURA, The Past Within, We Were Here, Keep Talking and Nobody Explodes). In all three groups, the clearest lesson is that puzzle and plot should be the same event. Thomas Grip of Frictional Games calls Inside a "playable story". The player does not start a level already driving a submarine. They hide from the man in it, sneak past him and steal it, which Grip says "is a lot more powerful" ([Frictional Games](https://frictionalgames.blogspot.com/2016/07/thoughts-on-inside-and-playable-stories.html)). The Gardens Between builds each island from the objects of one shared childhood memory. Its designers first list the setting's objects (lights switch on and off, see-saws move, radios play music) and only then decide how the puzzle rule affects each one ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-designing-the-beautifully-surreal-time-puzzles-of-i-the-gardens-between-i-)). Lost in Play worked backwards from the places its artists wanted to draw: "a goblin village sounds fun — how do we get there?" ([Apple Developer](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=n4w6zydm)). Planet of Lana II gives each region creatures whose abilities fit their home terrain, so the puzzles seem to grow out of the place ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/planet-of-lana-2-children-of-the-leaf-interview-art-direction-varied-environments-hybrid-robots-and-more)). For a team with strong illustrators this is a practical method. **List the places you want to paint, ask what real thing in each would stop two children, and ask which sibling's body gets past it.**

The second lesson is that the puzzle tool works best when it is also the story's key object, so the last puzzle can close the plot. In Creaks, the light that turns monsters back into furniture is also the light that defeats the giant monster at the climax ([Wikipedia: Creaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creaks)). In Bramble, a stone of light pushes back the brambles throughout and later saves the hero ([Wikipedia: Bramble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bramble:_The_Mountain_King)). Brothers goes furthest. Each thumbstick controls one brother, and near the end the younger brother, who could never swim, crosses water by pressing the dead older brother's button. Josef Fares calls that moment "the essence of the whole game" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons)). Fares' studio, Hazelight, later made a rule of this, "every situation has to reflect what goes on in the game". In It Takes Two, a couple drifting apart each get half of a magnet broken in two ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/feature/2021/02/12/splitting-up-together)), and "if you meet a couple of squirrels with some weird tic, you play that" ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2021/4/22/22396763/josef-fares-it-takes-two-interview-co-op-gaming)). Rewards can carry meaning instead of points. Never Alone's short documentary clips, unlocked through play, "received more critical praise than anything else in the game" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/postmortem-e-line-media-and-upper-one-games-i-never-alone-i-)). **In this game the treasure map is the natural key object.** It shows the goal, its pieces reward each chapter, and it can hold the hints, the clues and the final answer.

The failures are just as consistent. Grip criticises Inside's pressure switches and handy boxes as "abstract puzzle devices". He says sections that repeat an earlier puzzle without adding story "stick out like sore thumbs", and that the world "never forms a cohesive whole" because players rarely learn its layout ([Frictional Games](https://frictionalgames.blogspot.com/2016/07/thoughts-on-inside-and-playable-stories.html)). Digital Trends called It Takes Two "kitchen sink design", with plot threads that "come out of left field to justify moving the game to a snow-themed level" ([Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/gaming/it-takes-two-review)). PC Gamer blamed Bramble's "archaic puzzle design" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/bramble-the-mountain-king-review)), and a Lost in Play review complained of "too many sliding puzzles" ([Rock Paper Shotgun mirror](https://wwwrockpapers.pages.dev/posts/lost-in-play-review-a-gorgeous-point-and-click-puzzler)). Ron Gilbert's 1989 rules for adventure games already name the fixes. Every puzzle should move the story forward. The player should see the problem before the solution. There must be a visible reason why an action affects its target ([Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)). In a storybook family game, that means a pressure plate becomes a loose floorboard or a log see-saw. A door that opens must open because of a string, a weight or a latch the players can see. The map also answers Grip's complaint about layout, because it shows the children where they are in the world.

Character comes through in how each sibling handles the same thing, not just in cutscenes. Lost in Play's team found early versions "a little too cute" and added more teasing between the siblings and a fight halfway through ([Apple Developer](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=n4w6zydm)). In Brothers the younger boy tosses away a flower the older one stops to smell, and a matching flower near the end lets the player smell it "as his big brother did" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons)). A study of children aged 8-12 playing co-op games found that LEGO Star Wars produced the most shared laughter, and that the laughter came from story, funny character moments and shared goals ([TheNest recap of CHI 2010](https://sesame-nest.blogspot.com/2010/04/chi-2010-understanding-and-evaluating.html)). **Small, funny reactions that differ for each sibling are not decoration: they are what makes a family laugh together.**

## Teach it alone, use it together, twist it, then retire it

### Teaching without words

Almost every single-player story game studied tells its story and teaches its puzzles without text. The teaching comes from where things are placed. Limbo's players did not realise they could pull a boat, so the team put the boy right next to it and held him there until players learned the action. A bear trap that pulled attention away from a spider was moved to another screen. When testers missed a solution, the team added earlier scenes that taught it. Designer Jeppe Carlsen adds two rules. The right solution should be easy to carry out once understood, and wrong approaches should clearly *look* wrong ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/gdc-europe-i-limbo-i-s-carlsen-on-making-players-your-worst-enemy-and-your-best-friend)). Inside "doesn't have any superfluous actions", so players can quickly ask "can I interact with it?", and the boy's sneaking animation tells players when to be careful ([Frictional Games](https://frictionalgames.blogspot.com/2016/07/thoughts-on-inside-and-playable-stories.html)). Machinarium shows goals in picture thought bubbles ([Wikipedia: Machinarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machinarium)), and its clues are meant to be fairly obvious, such as a "No robots" sign marking where the robot should go ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/interview-amanita-design-s-dvorsky-on-i-machinarium-i-s-eerie-adventure)). In Little Nightmares II the companion Six points at objects of interest when the player is lost ([Press Start](https://press-start.com.au/reviews/playstation4-reviews/2021/02/10/little-nightmares-ii-review-misery-loves-company/)). Valve swapped a confusing shimmering force field in Portal's first room for plain glass ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success)). Puzzle designer Brett Taylor explains why this matters for beginners. New players have far less working memory to spare, and anything on screen that doesn't help them is "noise" ([Hamatti notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/Gaming/Puzzle-game-design)). For kids and non-gamers, the lesson is a tiny set of actions per sibling, one consistent look for every brother-sized gap, one for every sister-height hook, and nothing extra in the first room where an idea is taught.

### Escalating across a chapter and a whole story

Nintendo's level design gives the clearest way to make puzzles harder over time. It uses a four-beat structure borrowed from East Asian storytelling. A new idea is introduced where failure is hard, developed with hazards, given a twist, and then dropped. In Super Mario 3D World the whole cycle takes about five minutes per level ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2015/03/17/super-mario-3d-world-design/); [MCV/Develop](https://www.mcvuk.com/development/video-nintendos-level-design-secrets-in-four-steps)). The first beat must be gentle. Blizzard saw a young player quit The Lost Vikings after falling into an electric pit on the first level, and made safe, easy openings a goal from then on ([Blizzard News](https://news.blizzard.com/en-gb/article/23609765/the-lost-vikings-and-how-we-learned-to-love-multiplayer-puzzles)). Fares found Brothers' early puzzles too hard and decided that "every puzzle and challenge needed to be quite soft" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons)). Planet of Lana II's team warns that adding **too many new elements at once can make a puzzle "much more difficult than intended"**, so they test each puzzle in its place in the full game ([Xbox Wire](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2026/03/03/planet-of-lana-ii-puzzle-design/)). Never Alone shows a good twist. Wind starts as a danger the girl braces against and later becomes something to ride across gaps, and Engadget called riding the gusts one of the game's most enjoyable mechanics ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2014-12-10-never-alone-review-into-the-storm.html)). The same game also shows the main danger. Its makers admit "the difficulty level in the game spikes more significantly than we intended towards the end" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/postmortem-e-line-media-and-upper-one-games-i-never-alone-i-)).

Two more warnings apply directly to a sister-and-brother design. In Snipperclips, players kept reusing one shape to solve everything, so the designers built later puzzles that forced other shapes ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2017/11/feature_sfb_games_on_going_back_for_more_in_snipperclips_plus_cut_it_out_together)). "Sister lifts brother" could become that one shape, so some rooms must be built where it fails. Zelda's Water Temple is the classic water-level dungeon, and it was criticised for "exhausting repetition" because getting harder meant cycling through the same water states ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_Temple_(Ocarina_of_Time))). One analysis argues that letting players choose a height directly would have cut most of the backtracking ([Goomba Stomp](https://goombastomp.com/?p=114502)). Jonathan Blow's advice on where twists come from is to build puzzles on the "truth" of a mechanic, found by exploring it rather than deciding puzzles from the top down ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/indiecade-inside-jonathan-blow-s-puzzle-design-process)). The truths about tall and small are simple and rich. Tall reaches, sees over things, is heavier and can't fit. Small fits, hides, is lighter, sees what is low and can't reach. Each of those is a whole family of puzzles, and most of the menu below comes from them.

Games where each player sees different things get harder in their own way. In The FriendShip's puppet puzzle, rounds 1-5 have one describer, rounds 6-10 swap roles, and rounds 11-15 swap every step. Its trust course moves from plank paths to fake bridges to obstacles the runner cannot see at all ([Adventure Game Hotspot](https://adventuregamehotspot.com/review/596/we-were-here-expeditions-the-friendship)). The Past Within keeps things fresh by swapping the two players' views halfway through ([Adventure Game Hotspot](https://adventuregamehotspot.com/review/303/the-past-within)). Across this game's chapters, that suggests a natural curve. The House uses one-way relays (he sees, she does). The City swaps who reads and who acts. The Forest uses two-way exchanges, where each sibling holds half of the answer, plus a lookout guiding a walker who cannot see.

### Pacing puzzles against walking, spectacle and rest

Constant puzzles wear players down, and the studios say so directly. Creaks' lead designer adds story scenes and hidden paintings because they "bring the elements of relief and reward between each puzzle section" ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/creaks-interview-inspiration-development-challenges-future-updates-and-more)). Planet of Lana lets its two characters sit down to take in a view, but reviewers criticised long stretches of just pushing right through similar greenery ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2023/05/22/planet-of-lana-review/)). In Little Nightmares II, frequent instant deaths turned chases from tense to repetitive ([Press Start](https://press-start.com.au/reviews/playstation4-reviews/2021/02/10/little-nightmares-ii-review-misery-loves-company/)). Level designers describe pacing as contrast. A jump from calm to peak feels huge, while "10 to 11" barely registers ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/trinity-part-6-intensity-ramps)), and "breathers are content, not absence" ([Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/pacing-in-indie-games)). Spectacle is expensive and best used once. Hazelight built a plane fight players see "for one minute" and refused to reuse it ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2021/4/22/22396763/josef-fares-it-takes-two-interview-co-op-gaming)), and one Split Fiction dragon took about eight months to make ([Xbox Wire](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2025/03/05/split-fiction-josef-fares-interview/)). Fares also notes that co-op is harder to pace because "the other player could be on the total other side" ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2021/4/22/22396763/josef-fares-it-takes-two-interview-co-op-gaming)). Story moments therefore need gentle "both arrive" points. Gilbert adds that "real time is bad drama": timed scenes should give players slack. He also says each act should end at a single bottleneck, which gives a sense of completion ([Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck); [Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)). For a sense of scale, Inside runs about three hours ([CogConnected](https://cogconnected.com/feature/inside-turns-bad-game-design-head/)) and Planet of Lana about four ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2023/05/22/planet-of-lana-review/)). BOKURA's 3-4 hours was praised because it can be finished in one or two sittings ([SUPERJUMP](https://superjumpmagazine.com/bokura-review)).

## Co-op breaks down when each child just clears their own gate

The Lost Vikings stated the core rule in 1992: "the stages are built so that you can't cross them without using your Vikings to support each other" ([Blizzard News](https://news.blizzard.com/en-gb/article/23609765/the-lost-vikings-and-how-we-learned-to-love-multiplayer-puzzles)). The co-op games that work give each character an action the other lacks *and* a body the other can use, such as a boost, an anchor or a platform. They then chain those actions so one character's move opens room for the other. Brothers maps closely onto this project: the older brother pulls levers and lifts, and the younger one squeezes between bars ([Wikipedia: Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brothers:_A_Tale_of_Two_Sons)). The Shadow Puppeteer team calls the opposite of this the "tagalong trap", where one player becomes a sidekick. Their fix is to alternate who matters, "first one, then the other" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/avoiding-the-quot-tagalong-trap-quot-)). Research at the University of Waterloo found that designed differences between players can raise their sense of connection and engagement. The effect grows when designers tune who depends on whom, and when ([Waterloo Touch Lab](https://uwaterloo.ca/touchlab/references/asymmetry-and-beyond-improving-social-connectedness)).

Different abilities alone do not guarantee teamwork. Little Nightmares III gave one child a bow and the other a wrench, and several reviews described a rhythm of "you do your thing, I'll do mine". GamesRadar's verdict was that "co-op is a hindrance instead of the evolution it should've been" ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/platformer/little-nightmares-3-review)). One Unravel Two review estimated that **about 70% of its puzzles could be solved with one character** ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/review-unravel-two/)). Valve's test for Portal 2 was blunt: a co-op puzzle one person can solve alone does not pass ([AWN](https://www.awn.com/print/blog/talking-portal-2-valve-software-s-erik-wolpaw)). Helper roles also tend to fade. In Child of Light, the second player's puzzle role "tapers off severely" over the game ([Co-Optimus](https://co-optimus.com/review/1365/page/2/child-of-light-co-op-review.html)). There is a useful nuance from Lovers in a Dangerous Spacetime: forcing roles with colour-coded power-ups confused players, so the developers let people pick their own roles ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/co-op-communication-in-i-lovers-in-a-dangerous-spacetime-i-)). This game's roles come from bodies the players can see (tall, small), which is why they will make sense. Adding arbitrary "sister-only colour" locks on top would undo that.

Split information, where each player sees or knows something the other doesn't, has its own rules. We Were Here's studio says "if it doesn't get you talking, whispering, laughing or yelling then it shouldn't be in our games". Players should get stuck only when they stop cooperating, not because a puzzle is unclear ([Xbox Wire](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/?p=190237)). Keep Talking and Nobody Explodes says "it starts with balance": both sides need unique information to trade. Inputs should be "simple and deliberate", so failures come from decisions rather than dexterity. Its designers found confusing wording only by eavesdropping on players, and removed the capital O because it looked like a zero. They also saw that **kids enjoyed bossing their parents around** ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/finding-the-fun-in-bomb-defusal-with-i-keep-talking-and-nobody-explodes-i-)). BOKURA shows each player a differently drawn world, so one player sees a rope where the other sees a movable box ([Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/35252)). Its nine-symbol spot-the-difference gate was "nearly impossible without describing everything on screen" ([GameLuster](https://gameluster.com/early-access-review-bokura-sorry-ive-got-no-head/)), and a parent playing with an elementary-school child found the game too hard ([Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/35252)). Seeing the partner's screen undoes the whole idea. Escape Academy lets each player see the other's view, and a reviewer called the result "screen cheating" ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/?p=398998)). Information splits also suit families of mixed skill. Nintendo designed Super Mario Galaxy's helper mode for "situations like where a parent would be sitting by their child" ([Iwata Asks](https://www.nintendo.co.uk/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-Super-Mario-Galaxy/Volume-4-Shigeru-Miyamoto/1-The-Old-Issue-of-Two-Player-Play/1-The-Old-Issue-of-Two-Player-Play-220647.html)). A less nimble adult can hold the "knowing" role while the child does the moving.

The waiting player is the most common complaint. Players said The Past Within "sometimes drags when one player waits for the other to find clues", and some of its puzzles can be brute-forced by one player ([JumpDashRoll](https://www.jumpdashroll.com/article/the-past-within-review); [Adventure Game Hotspot](https://adventuregamehotspot.com/review/303/the-past-within)). BOKURA's final level kept one player's head parked on a button for about two of a seven-hour run, leaving her "with little control" ([GameLuster](https://gameluster.com/early-access-review-bokura-sorry-ive-got-no-head/)). Some players got stranded with "no way to progress or rewind" ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1801110/discussions/0/3777994239409722853)). Keep Talking itself came out of watching people grow bored while they waited for a turn on a VR ride ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keep_Talking_and_Nobody_Explodes)).

Communication tools are the gap most of these games leave open. BOKURA ships no in-game communication and tells players to use a call ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1801110)). In Portal 2 playtests, partners spent minutes just agreeing where to put a portal. Valve fixed it with a **ping tool** for marking spots and a small view of the partner's screen ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2012-03-08-the-importance-of-playtesting-what-portal-2-could-have-been.html)). Big Walk mixes nearby voice, radios and gestures. Its whiteboards were "really clever" but less effective than radios ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/review/big-walk/a-walk-to-remember)). We Were Here's one-at-a-time walkie-talkies "get to be tiresome" ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/?p=437414)). Little Nightmares III lets its pair call out and hold hands ([Inven](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/19667/little-nightmares-iii-interview-co-op-low-alone-and-the-terrifying-spiral-ahead-of-october-10-release)). Never Alone chose couch co-op partly because talking over a headset would break the mood ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/postmortem-e-line-media-and-upper-one-games-i-never-alone-i-)). A browser game for children in two different homes cannot assume voice chat, so the game has to supply simple, wordless ways to say "look here", "wait" and "go".

Online sync decides what is cheap to build. Sending only player inputs requires both machines to simulate identically, and everyone waits for the slowest connection. Sending game state instead is more forgiving but "approximate and lossy" ([Gaffer On Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/state_synchronization/); [Gaffer On Games](https://gafferongames.com/categories/networked-physics/)). Even a single-player team, The Gardens Between, replaced physics with fixed animations so that falling objects landed in the same place every time ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-designing-the-beautifully-surreal-time-puzzles-of-i-the-gardens-between-i-)). Unravel Two's rope swings were hard to coordinate even on one couch ([Nintendo Life](https://nintendolife.com/reviews/nintendo-switch/unravel_two)). This project's rule that **neither sibling carries anything** turns out to help. It removes carried objects, which are the main "medium" sync problem, and pushes puzzles toward world changes that are easy to broadcast: a gate opens, the water drops one step, a flower opens.

## A menu of original puzzles for the sister and brother

Every idea below is original to this game. The "Pattern from" column names the game whose general *pattern* inspired it, not something to copy. Three ratings follow. **Play** is how hard it is for a family pair. **Build** is the rough art and code cost. **Sync** describes online difficulty. Easy means on/off or step-by-step states that a server can broadcast. Medium means things that move on a script or a shared clock. Hard means free physics or both players pressing at the same instant. Several rows assume one small new action, a **boost**, where the sister lifts the brother onto a marked ledge. The last section recommends adding it. The current "look in" and "cheer" actions are reused rather than replaced.

### A strong set for Chapter 3: the waterfall and the old oak

The forest should teach two systems, **water level** and **sunlight**, combine them once, and then build to a vertical climax at the oak. It keeps the existing actions and adds only the boost. The sequence below follows the chapter template in the next section and runs about 19 minutes.

| Minutes | Beat | Puzzle(s) | Who does what | Story tie |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0-2 | Arrival | High marks, low marks (a single fork, very gentle) | She reads trunk carvings and he reads root marks | The new map piece shows a waterfall and an oak, and they follow the sound of water |
| 2-6 | Teach water | Three-step water: one lever lowers it one step, then raising it floats him up | She works the high sluice on an old stone weir; he rides the log up and drops a vine ladder for her (relay) | Someone long ago built this weir, the first hint about the map-maker |
| 6-7 | Twist water | Gauge on the wrong side, then roles flip: a tiny sluice inside the weir is brother-sized, and now she reads the tide marks | The reader becomes the doer | The builder left a small door "for a child" |
| 7-8 | Rest | Toys with no prize, leaf-boat race | Free play | Sibling teasing |
| 8-11 | Teach and combine light | Sunbeam path, then Pool as mirror | She clears the canopy, he angles the slate, and together they set the water to height two | Sleeping flowers open and the forest "wakes" for them |
| 11-14 | Separation peak | Lookout and walker on the wet ledges behind the waterfall, then Glow that follows the small one inside the grotto | She guides him from above, then in the dark he leads her | Cave paintings at two heights each give one sibling a symbol for the oak door |

### Later chapters: where the hunt could go next

These are options, since the story after the forest is still open. Each adds one new world system while the siblings' bodies stay the same.

| Possible chapter | New system | Puzzle ideas | Role in the sibling story |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under the Hill (caves past the waterfall) | Darkness and echo | The Echo hunt grows into an echo map: cheer from three spots, and the strongest echo marks the way. Two linked underground pools, where raising one lowers the other. Glow-worm escorts deeper in. Cave paintings that tell the map-maker's story as storybook pages. | The brother leads more often |
| The Windy Hill (dusk, an old windmill) | Wind and sky | Windmill sails with four stop positions act as a giant clock: she works the brake up high, and he sees which notch from the gear room. | Home Again (the finale) | Everything, turned around | The brother now holds the whole map and guides the sister through their dark house. The final lock asks for one symbol from every chapter. The finale structure rests on two findings. Brothers shows that one late twist on a long-established action carries more feeling than any cutscene ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons)). Big Walk shows that solving puzzles can reveal new features that change how players see places they already know ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/review/big-walk/a-walk-to-remember)). A treasure that changes how the children see their own home, such as something a parent hid there as a child, would use both. That treasure idea is a suggestion for the story, not a research finding.

|---|---|---|---|
| Box code chalked in the dark cupboard | One-way relay: he looks in, she enters the code | Crossed wires in the forest, with codes that change each game; the dark small space returns as the glow-worm grotto | Another fixed code passed one way |

| Wind tears the map into pieces | The map as goal and reward | Pieces show different marks to each sibling, and the finale needs every half | Pieces that are only collectibles |
| Pedestrian crossing, traffic lights on the avenue | Read a signal before moving | Lookout and walker that reads nature's signals: leaves bend before a gust, foam marks slippery stones | A third "read the lights" puzzle |
| Holding the shutter button for each other | Hold-and-go | Hold and watch, where the holder also signals | Holding as pure waiting |
| Brother crawls under the gate to open it | One sibling opens the way | Relay ladder and the oak climb, where every step changes hands | Openings that always go his way |
| Cornering the runaway puppy | Animals follow simple rules | Call and shoo; the puppy's optional return | Another two-sided pincer chase |
| Canal ferry with a button on each bank | A two-station machine | Three-step water with a floating log; gauge on the wrong side | Another "one button each side" |

One warning about the City draft. Four of its puzzles are about reading signals or maps: the crossing, the traffic lights, the metro wall map and the bus route map. Players criticised repeated puzzle types in Lost in Play's sliding puzzles ([Rock Paper Shotgun mirror](https://wwwrockpapers.pages.dev/posts/lost-in-play-review-a-gorgeous-point-and-click-puzzler)) and in Little Nightmares III's sameness ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/platformer/little-nightmares-3-review)). Merging the crossing and the traffic lights into one beat, or turning one of the map puzzles into a physical one, would help. The forest can then lean on nature systems and bodies rather than reading.

## A chapter template, a per-puzzle checklist and the safety nets

### The shape of a 15-20 minute chapter

The template below is my own synthesis of the sources cited in each row. It is not a published formula.

| Minutes | Beat | Intensity | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0-2 | Arrival | Low | A story beat, and the map shows the goal and the first small step | A clear end goal and first sub-goal hook players ([Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)) |
| 2-7 | Cycle 1: idea A | Rising | Show it alone, use it together, small twist | The four-beat cycle ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2015/03/17/super-mario-3d-world-design/)) |
| 7-8 | Breather | Low | Toys, a joke, a contest, a sit-down | "Breathers are content" ([Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/pacing-in-indie-games)) |
| 8-12 | Cycle 2: idea B, then B with A | Rising | One new idea, then combine it with A | One new element at a time ([Xbox Wire](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2026/03/03/planet-of-lana-ii-puzzle-design/)) |
| 12-15 | Separation peak | High | A short split-information puzzle with the siblings apart; roles swap once | Talking is the puzzle ([Xbox Wire](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/?p=190237)); separation should be used with caution ([Chalmers](https://odr.chalmers.se/bitstreams/c80d6e29-596c-4187-b2d7-01a00b7733bd/download)) |
| 15-18 | Finale set piece | Peak | Easy to do, big to look at, secretly waits for the slower player | "Real time is bad drama" ([Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)); spectacle used once ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2021/4/22/22396763/josef-fares-it-takes-two-interview-co-op-gaming)) |
| 18-20 | Reward | Low | Map piece, story reveal, a rest moment; both siblings gather here | Acts end at one bottleneck ([Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)) |

### Teaching and escalating one idea inside the chapter

Each idea gets four beats. **Show it alone.** Use a dead-end corner with nothing else to touch, where nothing can go wrong, as Limbo did with the boat ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/gdc-europe-i-limbo-i-s-carlsen-on-making-players-your-worst-enemy-and-your-best-friend)). **Use it together**, so the other sibling's action is needed to make it pay off. **Twist it** by breaking an assumption (the wrong sibling) or by turning a danger into a tool (the wind that later lifts him). **Retire it**, or fold it into the finale, instead of cycling it again like the Water Temple. In the forest, water works like this: one lever, one step, tide marks visible (show). Then she raises the water so he floats up and drops her a ladder (together). Then the small sluice inside the weir makes him the operator while she reads the marks (twist). Finally the water rises one last time under them in the climb (finale). Never introduce a new sunlight element and a new water element in the same puzzle.

### Tying every puzzle to a story beat

Gilbert's rules make a practical test. Each puzzle should answer "why would two children have to do this, here, now?" Both players should see the problem before the solution. The cause should be drawn on screen. Solving it should change the story state: a door opens, the map grows, an animal becomes a friend ([Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)). Plan each chapter backwards from its map piece as a **puzzle dependency chart**, a diagram that shows what must be done before what. Gilbert's charts show requirements, not the order players experience them in. They should be "bushy": one solved puzzle opens two or three side-by-side ones that later rejoin ([Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)). For co-op, I suggest tagging every step on the chart as S (sister), B (brother), S+B (both at once) or I (needs information from the partner). That tagging is my own extension, since no published co-op chart was found. Side-by-side branches should go to *different* siblings, so both work at the same moment. More than two single-sibling steps in a row is a sign that one player is idle, and each chapter should split critical actions roughly evenly. Gilbert recommends software that lays the chart out automatically and notes that his tool is built on Graphviz, which works from plain text, so the charts can live as text files beside the level data ([Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)).

The siblings' relationship can be the thing that grows across chapters. The House lets the calm, careful sister lead. The City trades roles. In the Forest the brother leads her through the dark. The finale turns the roles around completely. That arc, not rising difficulty, is what each chapter's puzzles should express.

### Hints, failure and getting stuck

The best hint systems step from a nudge to the answer, sit inside the story world, and add a little friction. Machinarium gives a free picture hint, then a sketched walkthrough of the current screen only, unlocked by a tiny mini-game, "so it is less convenient" to use as a full solution ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/interview-amanita-design-s-dvorsky-on-i-machinarium-i-s-eerie-adventure)). Lost in Play's picture hints "present challenges in themselves" ([Apple Developer](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=n4w6zydm)), Unravel Two has three stages ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/?p=316595)), and Return to Monkey Island's hint book "progressively exposes the deduction steps" ([The Arcade Artificer](https://jboger.substack.com/p/monkeyisland)). Creaks shipped with no hints and was criticised for it ([Wikipedia: Creaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creaks)), and its team planned numbered scenes so players could help each other online ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/creaks-interview-inspiration-development-challenges-future-updates-and-more)). Nintendo's Super Guide appears after repeated failure ([Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Super_Guide)), and Miyamoto says its appearance makes him "inspired to finish the level on my own" ([Vooks](https://vooks.net/miyamoto-reflects-on-the-super-guide)). For this game, the hints can live as doodles in the map's margins. Tier one restates the goal with a tiny picture of who might help (a small brother next to a gap). Tier two sparkles the place. Tier three shows who does what. Tier four is a "show me" ghost demonstration or a skip. Either player can open tiers one and two. Going further needs both to agree, so one impatient player can't spoil it for the other. That consent rule is my suggestion, since the notes found no research on hints for co-op pairs. For information puzzles, the hint should go to the sibling who is *missing* the insight, which pushes them to ask the right question.

Failure should be quick, cheap and funny. Limbo's "trial and death" made sense for an adult horror game, but even there "trial-and-error wears down on the player's stamina" ([Wikipedia: Limbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limbo_(video_game)); [Frictional Games](https://frictionalgames.blogspot.com/2016/07/thoughts-on-inside-and-playable-stories.html)). It Takes Two respawns a fallen player quickly ([Wikipedia: It Takes Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It_Takes_Two_(video_game))). Machinarium's designer says "it must be funny to fail, basically" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/interview-amanita-design-s-dvorsky-on-i-machinarium-i-s-eerie-adventure)), and Snipperclips aimed for mistakes that are funnier than the right answer ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2017/03/feature_snipperclips_-_working_with_nintendo_to_create_a_switch_co-op_classic)). Blizzard cut an auto-follow feature because dying while following someone else felt unfair ([Blizzard News](https://news.blizzard.com/en-gb/article/23609765/the-lost-vikings-and-how-we-learned-to-love-multiplayer-puzzles)). In practice: no lives and no game over. A slip puts the sibling back beside their partner within a second or two, and only that small puzzle resets, not the room. The server saves after every solved step, so a dropped connection restores both players to the same point.

### Randomising answers without breaking the story

The proven pattern is **fixed logic, random values**. Myst's remake offers optional random solutions where "how you get to the solution will be the same" ([ProGameGuides](https://progameguides.com/general-walkthroughs/myst-remake-walkthrough)). Silent Hill 2's codes "are random every time" ([Silent Hill Memories](https://www.silenthillmemories.net/sh2/puzzles_en.htm)). Keep Talking generates every bomb against a fixed manual ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keep_Talking_and_Nobody_Explodes)). BOKURA's fixed answers ended up shared openly on Steam ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1801110/discussions/0/3777994239409722853)). The city bus map already does this. Extend it to every information puzzle: codes, which stone differs, which knotholes are eyes, the order of symbols at the door. Keep level layouts and ability puzzles hand-made so the teaching order stays intact. Draw values from hand-checked sets of easy-to-name pictures (fox, moon, acorn), never look-alikes. Fix the values once per chapter and save them on the server so reconnects keep the same answers. The server should send each device only its own half. On a replay, which sibling gets which half of each clue can change, because the bodies cannot swap the way The Past Within's two roles do ([JumpDashRoll](https://www.jumpdashroll.com/article/the-past-within-review)). Randomising also stops an older child who has played before from simply calling out the answers.

### Testing with real families

Planet of Lana II's team works in a set order. They sketch first and check whether the goal is clear and whether failure tells players enough. They build a plain grey version, test it with people who have never seen the sketch, in full-game order, and add art and lighting last ([Xbox Wire](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2026/03/03/planet-of-lana-ii-puzzle-design/)). We Were Here's studio first tested ideas with real walkie-talkies and people in separate rooms ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-total-mayhem-games-i-we-were-here-i-)). That suits this game cheaply: two people, two rooms, paper versions of each screen. Listen to the conversation, because players never report the confusion you can hear ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/finding-the-fun-in-bomb-defusal-with-i-keep-talking-and-nobody-explodes-i-)). Watch for the five behaviours researchers used with children: laughing together, working out a plan, helping, waiting, and getting in each other's way ([TheNest](https://sesame-nest.blogspot.com/2010/04/chi-2010-understanding-and-evaluating.html)). Include parent-and-child pairs on purpose, given BOKURA's experience. Expect to throw a lot away: Limbo's makers "trashed 70%" of what they built ([Wikipedia: Limbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limbo_(video_game))).

## Six open design questions, answered

### Abilities: keep the bodies fixed and let the world change

Keep the sister's and brother's core abilities fixed for the whole game, because those abilities *are* the characters. The Lost Vikings kept three fixed characters across 37 levels ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lost_Vikings)). Brothers never changed its roles and relied instead on changing "the experience all the time" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons)). The risk of fixed abilities is repetition, which is what reviewers held against Little Nightmares III's one-tool-each design ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/platformer/little-nightmares-3-review)). The other extreme is Hazelight's: brand-new abilities in almost every level. It worked commercially, but it is very expensive ("not only do we have to create them, we have to polish them") ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2021/4/22/22396763/josef-fares-it-takes-two-interview-co-op-gaming)), and critics called it "kitchen sink" design ([Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/gaming/it-takes-two-review)). Split Fiction's middle path keeps the same basic movement and adds a different layer each level ([Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/gaming/split-fiction-review)). The siblings carry nothing, so here that layer should live in the world: the ferry and lift bridge in the city, water and sunlight in the forest, darkness and echo in the caves. On top of that, allow at most one small growth at a story beat, using an action the players already have. For example, in the forest the brother's cheer becomes a call that animals answer. That echoes Never Alone's fox, which gains a calling ability and becomes "a more vital character" ([CGMagazine](https://www.cgmagonline.com/review/never-alone-xbox-one-review)), and Samorost 3's flute, used to solve puzzles and help creatures ([Wikipedia: Samorost 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samorost_3)). The trade-off is that the level designers must keep inventing new uses for the same few actions. That is cheaper than new powers, but it needs discipline.

### Jumping: leave it out and add a boost

Leave out a free jump button. Add a context **boost**, where the sister lifts the brother onto marked ledges, plus marked step-up spots. The Lost Vikings made only Erik able to jump, which created strong gates between the characters ([Blizzard News](https://news.blizzard.com/en-gb/article/23609765/the-lost-vikings-and-how-we-learned-to-love-multiplayer-puzzles)). By the same logic, giving both siblings a free jump would wipe out every "lift me up" puzzle and weaken the sister's reach. Precise jumps also clash with the family audience. "Knowing the solution but not being able to execute it sucks" ([Hamatti notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/Gaming/Puzzle-game-design)), and Keep Talking deliberately uses "simple and deliberate inputs" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/finding-the-fun-in-bomb-defusal-with-i-keep-talking-and-nobody-explodes-i-)). Free jumping between two remote players is also the hardest kind of thing to sync. The evidence here is thin: the notes found no source that directly compares games with and without jumping, beyond point-and-click games like Lost in Play that do well without one. The trade-off is a less physical feel, and some children will expect a jump. If testing shows that, add a small decorative hop that never crosses a gap.

### Communication: pings and picture stickers built in

Build in four tools. First, **pings** that appear in the partner's *own* version of the world. Second, a small set of **picture stickers**: look, go, wait, yes, no, question and cheer. Third, **automatic sibling callouts**, such as the brother turning his head and pointing at a gap he fits through. Fourth, optional voice. Never show the partner's screen. Portal 2's ping tool was the fix for minutes lost agreeing on "over there" ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2012-03-08-the-importance-of-playtesting-what-portal-2-could-have-been.html)). Big Walk found gestures and radios beat whiteboards ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/review/big-walk/a-walk-to-remember)). Little Nightmares III's call and hand-holding carried the relationship ([Inven](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/19667/little-nightmares-iii-interview-co-op-low-alone-and-the-terrifying-spiral-ahead-of-october-10-release)). Escape Academy shows what happens when players can see each other's view ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/?p=398998)). Leaving out free text chat is my own judgement, for child safety and to keep reading low. The notes contain no evidence on it. The trade-off is that stickers limit nuance. For young players that is a feature, as long as every clue is designed to be sayable with stickers.

### Failure: soft, quick and funny

No lives and no game over. Respawn next to the partner within a second or two, reset only the small puzzle, make timed scenes wait for the slower player, and make fails comic. The evidence is in the hints-and-failure section above. The trade-off is less tension, which a family storybook game does not need. Keep "both start over" resets, which Pico Park players find fun ([Wasdland](https://www.wasdland.com/game/pico-park-1509960)), only for tiny comic rooms.

### Hints: tiered notes in the map's margins

Use the four tiers described above, kept in the map's margins. Both players must agree to go past tier two, and a skip is offered only after repeated failure. Count hint use per puzzle in testing. If half or more of the pairs need a strong hint, redesign the puzzle ([Room Escape Artist](https://roomescapeartist.com/2019/10/24/escape-room-hint-systems/)). Number the scenes so families can find help easily. The trade-off is that some pairs will lean on hints. That is better than the alternative Lost in Play's team named: being "stuck for months" or leaving to read a walkthrough ([Apple Developer](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=n4w6zydm)).

### Length: about five chapters, roughly ninety minutes

Plan about five chapters of 15-20 minutes: House, City, Forest, one more journey chapter, and a finale back home. That comes to roughly 80-100 minutes. Each chapter holds about ten to fourteen puzzle moments, arranged as three or four teach-use-twist cycles plus a set piece and a final lock. Comparable games are short and praised for it. BOKURA's 3-4 hours suits one or two sittings ([SUPERJUMP](https://superjumpmagazine.com/bokura-review)). The FriendShip runs about two hours ([PlayStation LifeStyle](https://www.playstationlifestyle.net/review/891396-we-were-here-expeditions-the-friendship-review-ps5/)) and scored 90% from Adventure Game Hotspot ([Adventure Game Hotspot](https://adventuregamehotspot.com/review/596/we-were-here-expeditions-the-friendship)). One pair finished Tick Tock in 80 minutes ([The Escape Roomer](https://theescaperoomer.com/tick-tock-a-tale-for-two/)). There is a floor, though. Unravel Two was called "fairly short" at seven story levels ([GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/you-and-me-till-the-end-unravel-two-review/)), and a Biped reviewer felt that "as you really begin to get a feel for one puzzle, it ends" ([Finger Guns](https://fingerguns.net/reviews/2020/04/14/biped-review-rentped/)). Finishing matters too: Fares said A Way Out's 51% completion rate "saddens me" ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/feature/2021/02/12/splitting-up-together)). Chapters a family can finish in one sitting protect completion. The notes found no data on the ideal length for family co-op, so this is a judgement. Prototype about twice as many puzzles as you plan to ship.

| Question | Recommendation | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Abilities | Fixed bodies; each chapter adds one world system; at most one small growth (cheer becomes a call) | Designers must keep finding new uses for the same actions |
| Jumping | No free jump; add a context boost and marked step-ups | Less physical feel; some children expect to jump |
| Communication | Pings in the partner's own view, picture stickers, automatic callouts, optional voice, no shared screen | Building a ping system; less nuance than chat |
| Failure | Soft, quick, funny; small local resets; restore on reconnect | Less tension |
| Hints | Four tiers in the map margins; both agree past tier two; skip only after repeated failure | Some pairs overuse hints, so track the rates |
| Length | About five chapters of 15-20 minutes (80-100 minutes total) | Some players may find it short; one-sitting chapters suit families |

## Conclusion

The game's two constraints, carrying nothing and (as recommended) not jumping, look like limits. In fact they match what the research says works. Puzzles become changes to the world rather than juggling objects. Those changes read clearly in storybook art, avoid "I know the answer but can't do it" moments, and are the easiest kind of state to keep in sync between two browsers. The real risk is repetition, not the constraints. Fixed actions need a world that keeps changing, so each chapter's new system, such as water and sunlight in the forest, does the job Hazelight's gadgets do at a fraction of the cost. The City draft's heavy lean on map-reading is the first place to watch for this.

Across the story, what should build is the siblings' relationship, not puzzle difficulty. The sister leads in the house, roles trade in the city, the brother leads through the dark in the forest, and the finale turns the roles around. Brothers shows that one late twist on an action players have known for hours lands harder than any cutscene. The treasure map is the thread that ties it all together. It states each goal, holds the hints, stores the symbols, changes the answers each game, and can end as the one thing only both siblings together can read.
