# Puzzle and Level Design Methods for Story-Driven Co-op Puzzle Games

Research notes, compiled 19 Nov 2026. Purpose: methods for planning, teaching, pacing and escalating puzzles in a 2-player online co-op browser puzzle-platformer (older sister reaches high things, younger brother fits through gaps; split abilities plus split information; house / city / forest chapters of 15-20 min; family audience). Every claim in "Cited Findings" has an inline source. "Inferences" are my own applications to this game and are not sourced claims. Several primary sources (GDC Vault recordings, the original Gamasutra Hayashida interview, the full Rocha 2008 and Seif El-Nasr 2010 papers, the GMTK video transcripts) could not be retrieved directly; where I rely on secondary write-ups this is flagged.

## 1. Teaching structures: kishōtenketsu, introduce-develop-twist-conclude, safe introduction, and the "aha" moment

### Takeaway
The best-documented teaching method is Nintendo's four-beat cycle: introduce a mechanic where failure is cheap, develop it, twist it, then drop it. Alongside it is the puzzle-designer consensus (Blow, Mark Brown, Arvi Teikari, Brett Taylor) that a good puzzle has one hidden insight. It is built from the "truth" of a mechanic, stripped of noise, and set up so the obvious assumption fails. Players learn by solving minimal puzzles, not by reading tutorials.

### Cited Findings

**Kishōtenketsu / Nintendo four-step level design (Koichi Hayashida, Super Mario 3D Land / 3D World; Mark Brown, GMTK)**
- Hayashida, director of Super Mario 3D Land, told Gamasutra that its level structure is based on the four-act East Asian narrative form called kishōtenketsu (ki = introduction, shō = development, ten = twist, ketsu = conclusion). Each level is "a self-contained set of challenges built around a mechanic unique to that level." This is reported second-hand in a Game Developer level-design breakdown; I could not retrieve the original interview. — [Game Developer, "Lessons from Suzy Cube"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/lessons-from-suzy-cube-level-design-breakdown)
- How the four steps work in practice, as summarised from Mark Brown's GMTK video on Super Mario 3D World: (1) **Introduction:** a new mechanic is taught "not with a series of terrible tutorials, but gameplay," in a low-risk space where failure is hard. (2) **Development:** the mechanic is expanded and combined with level geometry or hazards. (3) **Twist:** the idea is turned in an unexpected direction. (4) **Conclusion:** once mastered, the level drops the mechanic and the next level brings a new one. Brown notes the whole cycle takes about five minutes per level in 3D World. — [Engadget, "This is why Mario levels are brilliant" (2015)](https://www.engadget.com/2015/03/17/super-mario-3d-world-design/); [MCV/Develop, "Nintendo's level design secrets in four steps"](https://www.mcvuk.com/development/video-nintendos-level-design-secrets-in-four-steps)
- Sources differ on whether the four-act structure was fully formalised by 3D Land or matured by 3D World. — [MCV/Develop](https://www.mcvuk.com/development/video-nintendos-level-design-secrets-in-four-steps); [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/lessons-from-suzy-cube-level-design-breakdown)
- Kotaku, quoting the Gamasutra interview, links the "surprising twist" idea to Shigeru Miyamoto's background drawing four-panel comics. — [Kotaku, "How drawing comics helped Shigeru Miyamoto make great Mario levels"](https://kotaku.com/how-drawing-comics-helped-shigeru-miyamoto-make-great-m-5901805)

**Jonathan Blow (Braid, The Witness): puzzles come from the "truth" of a mechanic**
- Blow finds puzzles by exploring a mechanic, not by deciding top-down what puzzles he wants. In The Witness, ruling out self-crossing lines produced the region-partition puzzles: "that's something that came from exploration, not from a top-down decision about what I wanted the puzzles to be." — [Game Developer, "IndieCade: Inside Jonathan Blow's puzzle design process"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/indiecade-inside-jonathan-blow-s-puzzle-design-process)
- Method: create a situation, examine the underlying "truth" of the situation, and build the puzzle on that truth ("because the truth is valuable"). Each answer raises new questions, and the designer follows them. — [Game Developer (IndieCade)](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/indiecade-inside-jonathan-blow-s-puzzle-design-process)
- Completeness: push a rule through all its consequences. In Braid, time-immunity was tested against every object type ("I will get rid of fun if it means I can get at something deeper or more complete"). — [Game Developer (IndieCade)](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/indiecade-inside-jonathan-blow-s-puzzle-design-process)
- Surprise has to stay consistent with the rules: "Surprise is sort of the same thing as the information content of a message." Braid example: green-glowing keys are exempt from rewind, and a late puzzle uses a key that is not exempt. Blow admitted this "made sense" but was "very difficult to work out for the player." — [Game Developer (IndieCade)](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/indiecade-inside-jonathan-blow-s-puzzle-design-process)
- In The Witness, puzzles are recognisable as puzzles on sight ("when you came upon a puzzle, you knew instantly that it was a puzzle"). — [Game Developer (IndieCade)](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/indiecade-inside-jonathan-blow-s-puzzle-design-process)

**Mark Brown (GMTK, "What Makes a Good Puzzle?"): assumptions, catch, revelation**
- From a summary of the video (I could not fetch the transcript): the rules and limits of the mechanics form the puzzle, and the goal should be clear so the challenge is how to get there. A good puzzle has a **catch**, a logical contradiction where two things seem to conflict. Solving it is a **revelation**. Often the puzzle offers an obvious solution that fails, so the player must drop that **assumption** and rebuild their mental model. Example: Lara Croft GO first shows a tile mechanic as a way to deal with enemies, then makes it the wrong answer in the second half of the puzzle. Other points: "Puzzle should only have as many elements as are necessary to make it work," and difficulty should rise gradually, building on what the player has already learned. — [Hamatti notes, "Puzzle game design" (summary of GMTK video)](https://notes.hamatti.org/Gaming/Puzzle-game-design)
- The video features Snakebird and Stephen's Sausage Roll among its examples. — [search listing / Hamatti notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/Gaming/Puzzle-game-design)

**Arvi Teikari (Baba Is You, via GMTK) and Brett Taylor (Linelight)**
- Teikari works backwards from a solution or insight and builds the puzzle around it. Good puzzles often *prevent* actions rather than enable them. Baba Is You has no tutorial: it opens with puzzles so simple that players learn the mechanics because there is nothing else to do. — [Hamatti notes (summary of GMTK Baba Is You video)](https://notes.hamatti.org/Gaming/Puzzle-game-design)
- Brett Taylor ("Introduction to Puzzle Design"): beginners need far more working memory than experienced players. "Noise" is anything that uses up player memory without helping solve the puzzle, and red herrings are noise. His rules: "Simplify, simplify, simplify"; "Knowing the solution but not being able to execute it sucks"; write a "solution sentence" for each puzzle and find the simplest layout that expresses it; "Player perception is all that's real." — [Hamatti notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/Gaming/Puzzle-game-design)

**Teaching through ordering and presentation**
- Each puzzle should teach something new. Introduce components separately, ramp them up, then combine them with earlier mechanics ("no puzzle lives in isolation from the others"). Art should set expectations about how objects behave ("the single most important function of art in a puzzle game"). Show the whole puzzle and don't hide information ("a good puzzle wants to be solved"). Use the smallest solvable setup. — [Game Developer, "How to make a good puzzle: an explorable explanation"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-make-a-good-puzzle---an-explorable-explanation)
- Valve (Portal) simplified the environment until it taught clearly. The first room's shimmering force field confused players, so it became glass. Designers kept the button that needed the box in view and placed hints in the environment. — [Game Developer, "Best of GDC: The secrets of Portal's huge success"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success)

**Jesper Juul: failure as the engine of learning**
- Juul (*The Art of Failure*, MIT Press 2013) describes a **failure-improvement cycle**: the player meets a goal, fails in a way that reveals an inadequacy, searches for a solution, tries approaches until one works, and the cycle resets. Escaping that inadequacy is a central pleasure of games. Failure in games is personal because "you (not a character)" are inadequate. — [Big Think, "Play video games to fail"](https://bigthink.com/personal-growth/play-video-games-to-fail); [Penguin Random House book page](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/656618/the-art-of-failure-by-jesper-juul/)
- Secondary coverage reports that players prefer games where they feel responsible for failure over games where success is guaranteed. I could not confirm the underlying study. — [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/personal-growth/play-video-games-to-fail)

### Inferences
- **One mechanic per kishōtenketsu cycle, about 5 minutes each, gives 3-4 cycles per 15-20 min chapter.** For example, House: (ki) the sister lifts the brother onto a shelf with no danger nearby; (shō) lifting combined with a moving object such as a rolling toy car; (ten) the brother must lift something *for* the sister, or the sister's height becomes a problem (she can't fit under the stairs); (ketsu) a quick, confident use of the mechanic, then retire it or fold it into the finale.
- **Introduce each split ability in a solo-safe space first**, such as a dead-end room where only that player can act. Then introduce the *combination* in a second beat. Blow's "truth" test applies to the core pair: what is actually true about "tall vs. small"? Height reaches, size fits, height sees over things (information), and smallness hides. Each of these is a puzzle family.
- **Design the catch around a wrong assumption about who should act.** Players will assume the tall sister handles everything high. A good twist is a high target only the brother can reach, because he is lifted *and* fits through a gap at the top.
- **The "aha" must be shared.** In co-op, design the revelation so it lands on the player who holds the information, and so it has to be *spoken* to the other player to become useful (see Section 4).
- Treat Juul's failure-improvement cycle as the reason to keep failure cheap and readable. The family should see *why* they failed, for example the box tipped because the brother let go, so they can fix it together.

### Gaps
- "Teach, test, twist" as a named formula: I found no primary designer source for this exact phrase. It appears to be informal shorthand for the kishōtenketsu cycle.
- I could not retrieve the original Gamasutra Hayashida interview, his GDC 2012 talk, or the GMTK transcripts (Amara returned 403). Mark Brown's exact wording is reconstructed from third-party summaries.
- No primary source found for Jonathan Blow's "Truth in Game Design" talk text. The IndieCade write-up is the closest.

## 2. Puzzle dependency charts (Ron Gilbert) and chaining puzzles to story beats

### Takeaway
Plan puzzles backwards from each goal as a dependency graph, not a flowchart. Aim for "bushy" graphs where solving one puzzle opens two or three parallel ones that then converge. Break the game into acts that each end in a bottleneck. Gilbert's 1989 rules add that every puzzle must advance the story and have a clear, character-stated goal, with the problem shown before the solution.

### Cited Findings

**How to build a puzzle dependency chart (PDC)**
- Work backwards from the end of a puzzle chain, for example "The player needs to get into the basement." Then ask what that requires, and what each requirement requires. — [Ron Gilbert, "Puzzle Dependency Charts," Grumpy Gamer (2014)](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)
- Lines show dependency, not the order the player experiences: "Again, it's not flow, it's dependency." Independent puzzles are not connected. Gilbert's example is oiling the hinges and finding the key, which can be done in parallel and both feed "open the door." — [Gilbert, Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)
- A good chart is "bushy." Solving one puzzle should open two or three others, which then narrow back to a single solution that opens more. "There is nothing (NOTHING!) worse than linear adventure games." The chart shows sections that are too linear or too crowded, since too much choice is also a problem. — [Gilbert, Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)
- Design in **acts**, each ending in a bottleneck before the next. This gives a sense of completion and lets players file away what they know and clear out their inventory. — [Gilbert, Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)
- Story beats are generally left off the chart "unless they are critical to solving a puzzle." — [Gilbert, Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)
- Charts catch dead-end puzzles, uneven flow and bottlenecks. Gilbert says Maniac Mansion, built without charts, was "full of dead end puzzles." Monkey Island used them "for every aspect of the puzzle design." — [Gilbert, Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)
- Tooling: use software that re-lays-out the graph automatically. Gilbert used OmniGraffle and notes it is built on Graphviz, which takes text input. — [Gilbert, Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts). Puzzlon is a web-based PDC tool inspired by his post. — [IFWiki, Puzzlon](https://ifwiki.org/Puzzlon)
- Gilbert published Thimbleweed Park's PDCs during development, as act-by-act passes and a combined chart for Acts 1-3. These are real examples of a commercial game's charts. — [Thimbleweed Park blog, "Almost Final Puzzle Dependency Charts"](https://blog.thimbleweedpark.com/final_puzzles.html); [Thimbleweed Park blog, "Act 1, 2 and 3 Combined Puzzles"](https://blog.thimbleweedpark.com/act_123_puzzles.html)

**Tying puzzles to story: Gilbert's 1989 "Rules of Thumb"** ([Ron Gilbert, "Why Adventure Games Suck and What We Can Do About It," 1989, Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck))
- *End objective needs to be clear:* "At the beginning the player should have a clear vision as to what he or she is trying to accomplish."
- *Sub-goals need to be obvious:* reveal at least the first sub-goal, ideally through another character. "Letting the player know at least the first sub-goal is essential in hooking them."
- *Puzzles should advance the story:* "There is nothing more frustrating than solving pointless puzzle after pointless puzzle."
- *Backwards puzzles:* avoid finding the solution before the problem. Show the problem first so the solution clicks into place.
- *Unconnected events:* if an action unlocks something, there must be a logical reason ("make sure that there is a reason why those six objects would affect the door").
- *Incremental reward:* new areas, characters or plot revelations show the player "she is achieving."
- *Give the player options:* avoid "caging" players in linear puzzle sets. "Think of the player as outside the cages, and the puzzles as locked up within."
- *I forgot to pick it up:* never require an item picked up earlier unless it can be fetched again, or give it an immediate use.

**Modern application (Return to Monkey Island analysis)**
- Narrow an abstract story goal into a few concrete objectives (in RtMI, a mop and a disguise): "Here, we know exactly what to do, we just don't know how (yet)." Objectives come from the narrative, so the player knows both what to do and why. — [JB Oger, "Quest Design Lessons from Monkey Island," The Arcade Artificer](https://jboger.substack.com/p/monkeyisland)
- "Door & key" chains, such as keys hidden in chests, create a domino-effect payoff when the last key falls into place. Leftover dialogue redirects players, for example the map-maker asking "did the cook send you?" Providing several valid items (four different things work as a mop head) reduces dead searching. The aim is to "ensure players find the solution while still maintaining the illusion they figured it entirely by themselves." — [Oger](https://jboger.substack.com/p/monkeyisland)

### Inferences
- **The treasure map is the act structure.** Each chapter (House, City, Forest) is an act ending in a bottleneck, such as "find the next map fragment." The fragment reveal is the story beat and the reward Gilbert calls incremental. The map itself states the end objective in Gilbert's sense, and each fragment states the next sub-goal.
- **A co-op dependency chart needs an extra layer:** tag every node with *who* can do it (S = sister, B = brother, S+B = both at once, I = needs info from the other player). A healthy chapter alternates S, B and S+B nodes, and parallel branches should go to *different* players so both work at once (see Section 4). A run of more than two single-player nodes in a row on the critical path is a warning sign: the other player is idle.
- **Bushy is the co-op answer to waiting.** Gilbert's parallel branches (oil hinges / find key) map directly to "brother crawls through the vent to find the key while sister reaches the oil on the high shelf," then the two converge on the door.
- Draft charts as Graphviz/Mermaid text in the repo so they live beside the level data. That is the same text-input approach Gilbert recommends.
- Apply "no backwards puzzles" per player. Each player should see the *problem* before getting the solution. In split-information puzzles, the player holding the clue should already know a lock exists, or the clue will feel random.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the Game Developer analysis of Monkey Island 2's puzzle dependencies and balancing, a likely useful worked example: [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-puzzles-for-adventure-games-analyzing-monkey-island-2-puzzle-dependencies-and-balancing).
- I found no published PDC for a two-player co-op game. The "who can do it" tagging above is my own extension.

## 3. Pacing: intensity curves, alternating puzzle, traversal, spectacle and rest, chapter length, ending high

### Takeaway
Pacing is the rhythm of intensity against rest. Plan it as an explicit intensity plot per chapter, with rest periods treated as content, and use contrast, because a jump from calm to peak reads far stronger than 10 to 11. Swap mechanics often (Hazelight's "max variety," Nintendo's one-mechanic-per-level) and close each act with a bottleneck and a reward. Co-op is harder to pace because the two players may be in different places.

### Cited Findings
- Pacing is "the ratio of Intensity to Rest over the course of a segment of your game." Jesse Schell's "interest curve" is the same idea as an intensity ramp. Contrast matters more than absolute level: going from intensity 5 to 11 is a big jump, but 10 to 11 is not. — [Game Developer, "Trinity Part 6: Intensity Ramps"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/trinity-part-6-intensity-ramps)
- The Level Design Book defines pacing as "the general order and rhythm of activities in a level" and lists the intensity plot as a standard planning tool for set pieces. — [The Level Design Book, "Pacing"](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/preproduction/pacing)
- Map the game's sequence as a rough intensity graph to find flat stretches. "Breathers are content, not absence." — [Bugnet, "Pacing in indie games"](https://bugnet.io/blog/pacing-in-indie-games)
- World of Level Design (Pete Ellis) treats a level's intensity as a line graph built from gameplay beats and recommends raising difficulty across the level. — [World of Level Design, "Level design pacing: gameplay beats, part 2"](https://worldofleveldesign.com/categories/wold-members-tutorials/peteellis/level-design-pacing-gameplay-beats-part2.php)
- Puzzle games: a gradual difficulty ramp. The belief that the next puzzle will be harder creates tension of its own. — [Game Developer, "Reflections on difficulty pacing in Puzzledorf"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/reflections-on-difficulty-pacing-in-i-puzzledorf-i-)
- Nintendo's one-mechanic-per-level cycle runs about five minutes in Super Mario 3D World, and the mechanic is retired once mastered. — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2015/03/17/super-mario-3d-world-design/)
- Hazelight (It Takes Two): Fares promised maximum variety ("I think this game will take the world record in actually how much variation is in game") and argued that narrative games can become very repetitive. — [GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/narrative-games-can-become-very-repetitive-josef-fares-promises-max-variety-for-it-takes-two). "Whatever happens in the story will be reflected in the gameplay. If you meet a couple of squirrels with some weird tic, you play that." Pacing is harder in co-op: "It's easier to pace and control the player when it's single player," and "the other player could be on the total other side," so triggers are harder to place. — [The Ringer, Josef Fares interview (2021)](https://www.theringer.com/2021/04/22/video-games/josef-fares-it-takes-two-interview-co-op-gaming)
- Acts should end in bottlenecks that give a sense of completion. — [Gilbert, Grumpy Gamer](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts). "Real time is bad drama": use dramatic timing and give players slack in timed sequences. — [Gilbert 1989](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)
- Keep Talking and Nobody Explodes adjusts pacing on the fly. A hidden performance score adds events (an alarm, lights out) when players are doing well, and avoids piling on when they struggle: "We don't want to accelerate a death spiral; we want people to get those really exciting moments." — [Game Developer, "Finding the fun in bomb defusal"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/finding-the-fun-in-bomb-defusal-with-i-keep-talking-and-nobody-explodes-i-)
- Portal endings, good and bad: a chase sequence had "horrible" pacing and left players lost. The fire-pit / incineration beat felt climactic ("forcing the player to incinerate the box was a great satisfying ending"). Story was kept "wet" (flexible) and fitted to what players were already feeling. — [Game Developer, "Secrets of Portal's success"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success)

### Inferences
- **A possible 15-20 min chapter template** (my synthesis, not a sourced template):
  1. Arrival and orientation (1-2 min, low intensity): a story beat, then the map shows the chapter goal.
  2. Mechanic A, introduce and develop (4-5 min).
  3. Breather or traversal with a small discovery (1-2 min): a collectible, a visual gag, or a moment where the siblings interact.
  4. Mechanic B, introduce and develop, plus a twist that combines it with A (4-5 min).
  5. Short split-information puzzle where the players are briefly separated (2-3 min), the tension peak.
  6. Finale set piece: a fast, forgiving use of everything they've learned, with a spectacle payoff (2-3 min).
  7. Bottleneck reward: a map fragment and a story reveal, ending on a high (about 1 min).
- **Use contrast, not constant difficulty:** follow a hard split-information puzzle with an easy, physical, funny beat, such as the sister carrying the brother across a puddle.
- **Re-gather points:** because co-op pacing breaks when players spread out (Fares), use gentle "both arrive" gates before story beats so cutscenes fire with both present. Keep these gates short (see the Chalmers caution on separation gates in Section 4).
- **The finale should be easy to execute and hard to forget.** Per Gilbert's "real time is bad drama," any chase or timed finale should secretly wait for the slower player.
- Use Keep Talking-style dynamic intensity sparingly. For a family audience, *reduce* pressure when players struggle, never add it.

### Gaps
- No sourced industry guidance on ideal chapter length for family co-op games. 15-20 min is the project's own choice. I found no Hazelight primary data on chapter or level duration.
- No GDC talk from Hazelight on pacing was found. Fares' views come from interviews.

## 4. Co-op puzzle design principles: interdependence, carrying, parallel vs sequential, symmetric vs asymmetric, waiting, communication

### Takeaway
Co-op puzzles must be impossible for one player alone. Give each player a distinct ability or a distinct piece of information, and alternate who is critical so neither becomes a sidekick. Communication is the actual puzzle: make it the thing designers optimise for, and give players built-in tools (pings, a view of the partner's screen) so explaining *where* doesn't become a chore. Academic work (Rocha et al. 2008; Seif El-Nasr et al. 2010) gives a usable pattern vocabulary and observable metrics for playtests with children.

### Cited Findings

**Pattern vocabulary (academic)**
- Rocha, Mascarenhas and Prada, "Game Mechanics for Cooperative Games," Zon Digital Games 2008, pp. 72-80. They reviewed co-op design patterns and challenge archetypes in commercial games and built a two-player co-op game (later *Geometry Friends*) from them. — [GAIPS/INESC-ID publication record](https://gaips.inesc-id.pt/component/gaips/publications/showPublication/3/87); a 2014 DiGRA paper notes the work informally described six general patterns plus challenge archetypes — [DiGRA, "Game Design Patterns for Collaborative Player Interactions"](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/view/639)
- Seif El-Nasr, Aghabeigi, Milam, Erfani, Lameman, Maygoli and Mah, "Understanding and evaluating cooperative games," CHI 2010, pp. 253-262, DOI 10.1145/1753326.1753363. — [bibliographic record](https://bibtex.github.io/CHI-2010-El-NasrAMELMM.html); related thesis: [Aghabeigi, SFU MSc thesis](https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/sfu_migrate/11576/etd6462_BAghabeigi.pdf) (full text blocked, not read)
- Combined pattern list (first six from Rocha et al.; the rest added by Seif El-Nasr et al.), as summarised in a Game Developer article: **Complementarity** (different, complementary roles: The Lost Vikings). **Synergies between abilities** (one character modifies another's abilities). **Abilities usable only on another player** (TF2 Medic). **Shared goals**. **Synergies between goals** (separate but interdependent goals). **Special rules for teammates** (friendly fire). **Camera settings** (split screen / one character in focus / all in focus). **Interacting with the same object** (Beautiful Katamari, LittleBigPlanet). **Shared puzzles** (Lego Star Wars, LittleBigPlanet, Portal 2, Keep Talking). **Shared characters**. **Special characters targeting lone wolves** (Left 4 Dead Hunter/Smoker). **Vocalization** (automatic character callouts). **Limited resources**. — [Game Developer, Andrii Honcharuk, "Ready Player Two: co-op is a new big thing" (2017)](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/ready-player-two-co-op-is-a-new-big-thing-)
- **Cooperative Performance Metrics (CPMs)**, observable in playtest video: laughter or excitement together; worked-out strategies; helping (by controlling a character vs. by verbal instruction); waiting for each other; getting in each other's way. — [TheNest blog recap of the CHI 2010 talk](https://sesame-nest.blogspot.com/2010/04/chi-2010-understanding-and-evaluating.html)
- Study details: 14 co-op games, including LEGO Star Wars, LittleBigPlanet, Kameo, Rock Band, WALL-E, Kung Fu Panda and a Harry Potter game. The target group was children aged 8-12 (tested ages 6-16), with sessions videotaped and post-play interviews. LEGO Star Wars produced the most shared laughter (driven by narrative, funny character moments and shared goals) and the most worked-out strategies (shared puzzles, shared goals, complementary characters). It also produced the most "getting in each other's way," because its camera crowds all characters into one space. Waiting was high in LEGO Star Wars because all characters must gather before entering the next room. Kameo's 3D space was hard for younger kids, so peers helped them often. Conclusion: "the camera is very important" to cooperation. — [TheNest recap](https://sesame-nest.blogspot.com/2010/04/chi-2010-understanding-and-evaluating.html)
- A Chalmers thesis on three-player co-op uses shared puzzles, concurrency and **separation gates**, and reports that separation gates should be used with caution to avoid frustration. Only the abstract was read. — [Chalmers ODR thesis](https://odr.chalmers.se/bitstreams/c80d6e29-596c-4187-b2d7-01a00b7733bd/download)

**Avoiding the "tagalong trap" (one player carrying)**
- The Shadow Puppeteer team (Sarepta) names three components of "true co-op": (1) the game *relies* on two or more players, since "unless all players are integral to the completion of the game, there can be limited incentive to cooperate"; (2) each player has different roles and skills, because identical characters lead one player to do most of the work; (3) each player has equal importance, so "avoid having one feel like a 'sidekick'." — [Catharina Bøhler, "Avoiding the Tagalong Trap," Game Developer (2016)](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/avoiding-the-quot-tagalong-trap-quot-)
- Technique: **alternating dependency** ("first one, then the other"). Ibb & Obb restricts some portals to one character, so the players take turns being critical. Worked example from Shadow Puppeteer's "Home" level: the Boy climbs a 3D-only ladder the Shadow can't use. The Boy pulls a crate into the light so its shadow becomes a platform for the Shadow. Then a branch's shadow lets the Shadow advance, and the Shadow "must jump up, push the shadow of the crate, so the actual crate falls to create a platform for the Boy." The two share core verbs (walk, run, jump) but act on different targets: real objects vs. shadows. — [Bøhler, Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/avoiding-the-quot-tagalong-trap-quot-)
- Unravel Two shows the trap from the other side. Critics said "when 70% of the puzzles are solvable with a single Yarny, I begin to wonder why multiplayer is even present." Its combine mechanic lets one player absorb the other and carry the pair through hard platforming. — [Destructoid review (via search)](https://vip-develop.destructoid.com/?p=224565); [GamingTrend review](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/you-and-me-till-the-end-unravel-two-review/). Director Martin Sahlin said the game was "designed... right from the start [to be] about working together," and in single-player you control both Yarnys and must switch between them. — [EA, Unravel Two director interview](https://www.ea.com/en-us/news/unravel-two-director-interview-co-op) (page returned 404 on fetch; quote from search excerpt)

**Portal 2 co-op (Valve)**
- Erik Wolpaw: the team had to design co-op puzzles that one person couldn't do alone, and it "took the designers a little while to really wrap their heads around it." A co-op puzzle a single player can solve doesn't pass. — [AWN, "Talking Portal 2 with Erik Wolpaw"](https://www.awn.com/print/blog/talking-portal-2-valve-software-s-erik-wolpaw); [PC Gamer, "Interview: Valve on their insane Portal 2 ideas"](https://www.pcgamer.com/interview-valve-on-their-insane-portal-2-ideas)
- Playtests showed partners spending minutes just agreeing where to place a portal. The fix was environment tagging (the **ping tool**), which lets players flag surfaces and objects and start synchronised countdowns, plus picture-in-picture so each player can see the partner's view. — [Engadget, "The importance of playtesting: What Portal 2 could have been" (2012)](https://www.engadget.com/2012-03-08-the-importance-of-playtesting-what-portal-2-could-have-been.html); [Portal Wiki, Ping](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Ping). A Valve interview (exact outlet not confirmed from the search excerpt) quotes Chet Faliszek: "you could say 'Put a portal over there', 'Where's there?'. So we have the ping tool, picture-in-picture so you can see what they're seeing."
- An early competitive mode was cut after testing as "super chaotic but no fun." — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/valve-experimented-with-portal-2-competitive-multiplayer-ended-up-super-chaotic-but-no-fun)
- Academic framing: Portal 2's pings and avatar gestures are built-in collaborative communication mechanisms. — [University of Waterloo, GI 2016 paper](https://uwaterloo.ca/scholar/sites/ca.scholar/files/lnacke/files/gi2016-6.pdf)

**Split information / communication-first design**
- We Were Here (Total Mayhem Games): "every puzzle should be related in some way to communication" — describing symbols, guiding through a maze, mimicking sounds. "If it doesn't get you talking, whispering, laughing or yelling then it shouldn't be in our games." Stuck should mean a teamwork problem: "if players get stuck it should be because they're not cooperating or communicating well." Process: find the quickest test of a puzzle's core idea (cardboard, board-game parts, storyboards, origami, in-engine), and add art and story only after it works. "There can be months between that first version and something that looks like the final result!" Their sound puzzle began as a rhythm-matching challenge that proved too hard, moved to organic sounds, then added a creature to talk to and a dictionary held by the other player. — [Xbox Wire, "Co-op Puzzle Design for We Were Here Forever"](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/?p=190237)
- The design question Total Mayhem asks of each puzzle: "what will this make you explain?" / "what type of communication will this elicit?" — [Epic Games Store news](https://store.epicgames.com/news/we-were-here-forever-turns-brave-adventurers-into-voices-in-the-dark) (search excerpt). Puzzles are made deliberately "extra hard (read: fun) to describe." — [XboxAddict interview](https://xboxaddict.com/Article/433/We-Were-Here-Forever-Interview-with-Developers-at-Total-Mayhem-Games.html) (search excerpt)
- Keep Talking and Nobody Explodes (Steel Crate): the Defuser sees the bomb but not the manual, and Experts see the manual but not the bomb. "It starts with balance": each side holds unique information to trade. "We require simple and deliberate inputs," so failures come from decisions and communication, not dexterity. "Our game involves talking, so our job involved eavesdropping": players never *reported* the ambiguity of "cut the second wire" (from the top or the bottom?); designers found it only by listening. Kids enjoyed bossing their parents around, and parents enjoyed playing with their kids. — [Game Developer, "Finding the fun in bomb defusal"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/finding-the-fun-in-bomb-defusal-with-i-keep-talking-and-nobody-explodes-i-). Some modules deliberately use hard-to-describe glyphs and homophones. — [Wikipedia, Keep Talking and Nobody Explodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keep_Talking_and_Nobody_Explodes)
- KTANE's origin is a lesson about the idle player: at the 2014 Global Game Jam, people waiting for their turn on a VR rollercoaster weren't enjoying themselves. That led to "a game that could be shared alike by both a headset wearer and those watching the wearer." — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keep_Talking_and_Nobody_Explodes)
- BOKURA (tokoronyori): each player sees a *different* world. One sees a rope where the other sees a movable box, so they must talk to work out what differs between their screens. It is two-player-only, and solo play "differs from the intended design." The original reportedly sold about 600,000 copies. — [Automaton interview (BOKURA: planet)](https://automaton-media.com/en/interviews/interview-bokuras-developer-wants-to-see-players-fight-even-more-in-upcoming-sequel-bokura-planet/); [Automaton, "BOKURA: planet... loneliness"](https://automaton-media.com/en/interviews/bokura-planet-is-a-two-player-game-created-to-drive-people-who-arent-lonely-into-loneliness-we-ask-the-dev-why/)

**Hazelight (It Takes Two, A Way Out)**
- Co-op must be designed in from the beginning: "When you design a single-player game and then you add co-op to it, you have to adapt it." Mechanics should mean something together: "that the mechanic means something, and that it combines well with your partner." "The communication is key," and frustration shouldn't be built in. — [The Ringer, Fares interview](https://www.theringer.com/2021/04/22/video-games/josef-fares-it-takes-two-interview-co-op-gaming)
- It Takes Two set out to marry its many gameplay mechanics tightly to the narrative. — [Unreal Engine developer interview](https://www.unrealengine.com/developer-interviews/it-takes-two-lovingly-marries-story-and-gameplay-together) (403 on fetch; from search excerpt)

**Asymmetric skill: the parent-and-child case**
- Super Mario Galaxy's Co-Star mode was conceived for "situations like where a parent would be sitting by their child." Iwata observed that in other games "the second player is being forced to play, and it didn't feel that much fun." Some features were moved out of single-player into Co-Star to keep single-player clean. The co-star player uses the pointer to stop boulders and enemies for the less experienced player. — [Iwata Asks: Super Mario Galaxy, Vol. 4 (Miyamoto), "The Old Issue of Two Player Play"](https://www.nintendo.co.uk/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-Super-Mario-Galaxy/Volume-4-Shigeru-Miyamoto/1-The-Old-Issue-of-Two-Player-Play/1-The-Old-Issue-of-Two-Player-Play-220647.html); [Iwata Asks Vol. 1, "A Mario Even Beginners Can Play"](https://www.nintendo.co.uk/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-Super-Mario-Galaxy/Volume-1-The-Producer-and-Director/3-A-Mario-Even-Beginners-Can-Play/3-A-Mario-Even-Beginners-Can-Play-230106.html). Galaxy 2 expanded the helper's verbs: interrupting enemies, feeding star bits, throwing objects. — [Super Mario Wiki, Co-Star Luma](https://www.mariowiki.com/Co-Star_Luma). One critic called the lesser-role design a half-hearted add-on. — [Game Developer, "Mario Galaxy lights the way with Co-Star"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/mario-galaxy-lights-the-way-with-co-star)

### Inferences
- **Every critical-path puzzle needs both siblings.** Apply Valve's test literally: if one player could finish a puzzle alone by switching characters or brute force, redesign it. Allow optional solo-able side content only off the critical path.
- **Alternate who is critical, as in Shadow Puppeteer's "Home" level.** Example House sequence: the brother crawls through a cat flap and unlocks the back door → the sister reaches the high key hook and drops the key → the brother uses the key on a low cupboard only he fits into → inside is a step stool he pushes out so the sister can reach the attic hatch. Count beats per player, aiming for a roughly 50/50 split of critical actions per chapter.
- **Two axes of asymmetry, used separately and then together:** (1) *ability* (reach vs. fit) and (2) *information* (one sees the map, symbols or a view the other can't). For a family audience, introduce each axis alone before combining them.
- **Split information suits a skill gap.** Like KTANE's kids bossing their parents and Galaxy's co-star idea, a less dexterous player (a parent or non-gamer) can hold the *information* role: reading the map, spotting the pattern, guiding. The more dexterous player (often the child) handles traversal. Let the *pair* choose who plays which sibling, and keep each sibling's dexterity demands low.
- **Waiting time:** keep single-player-only segments to roughly 20-40 seconds and give the idle player something to do, such as holding a lever, watching for a pattern, or a cosmetic interaction. Show the partner's view (a mini picture-in-picture or a "partner camera" toggle) so the idle player can *advise*, which turns waiting into helping (a positive CPM). Treat separation gates with caution (Chalmers).
- **Parallel vs. sequential:** use *parallel* branches (from the bushy dependency chart) for flow, with each player solving their half at the same time, then converging. Use *sequential* hand-offs for drama and teaching. Use *simultaneous* actions (both pulling, one holding while the other moves) for set-piece peaks.
- **Communication tooling for an online browser game:** voice can't be assumed (kids, privacy, different houses). Build a ping/marker system ("look here," "go," "wait," "I've got it," a countdown) with contextual emotes. It plays the role of Portal 2's ping tool and the "vocalization" pattern (characters call out automatically, e.g. the brother says "I can fit through there!"). Make every split-information clue *describable with pings or a small set of symbols*, not only free speech.
- **Design for describability (KTANE / We Were Here):** pick clue alphabets that are fun but unambiguous for kids: colours, animals, simple shapes, counts. Avoid look-alikes (O/0), left/right ambiguity across two screens (use "the window side" or "the side by the clock"), and homophones for young readers.
- **Use the CPMs as a playtest checklist.** Watch for shared laughter, worked-out strategies, helping (physical vs. verbal), waiting, and getting in each other's way. Shared laughter came from narrative and funny character moments, so sibling banter and slapstick (the sister lifting a flailing brother) are worth building.
- **Camera:** for an online game each player can have their own camera, which avoids LEGO-style crowding. But design moments where both siblings share one view, so shared jokes and spectacle land together.

- The full texts of Rocha et al. 2008 and Seif El-Nasr et al. 2010 were not retrieved. The pattern and metric lists come from secondary summaries (Game Developer, a conference blog), and per-game numeric results were not available.
- No primary Coldwood design talk on Unravel Two's puzzle balance was found, and the EA interview page returned 404.

## 5. Difficulty and failure for families: hint systems, soft fail vs. restart, checkpoints, skips, accessibility

### Takeaway
Use optional, player-controlled, tiered hints that move from a nudge to the answer, ideally delivered in the fiction (Return to Monkey Island's hint book). Add a last-resort skip or show-me option after repeated failure (Nintendo's Super Guide). Make death and failure survivable and cheap (Gilbert's "live and learn"; "real time is bad drama"), and use hint-request rates as a diagnostic for broken puzzles.

### Cited Findings
- **Return to Monkey Island (2022) hint book:** Gilbert changed his mind on hints because stuck players "would jump to the web and read a walkthrough." He wanted hints to be "more than just a walkthrough." — [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/return-to-monkey-island-will-have-a-hint-system-to-help-out-stuck-players); [NME](https://www.nme.com/news/return-to-monkey-island-to-include-a-built-in-hint-system-3211952). The book starts with a slight nudge about where to look, gets more specific on repeated requests, and finally tells you what to do. It works as "a super-dialogue tree that progressively exposes the deduction steps," where "the player decides how many hints (and how precise)." — [JB Oger, Arcade Artificer](https://jboger.substack.com/p/monkeyisland). The hint book is separate from the difficulty modes (a casual mode similar to Monkey Island 2's). — [Legend of MI, Ars Technica interview summary](https://legendofmi.com/news/ron-and-dave-get-interviewed-by-ars-technica-727)
- General hint-system practice: hints should be optional (opt-in) and escalating (minimal help first). Different players need different amounts, from ignoring hints to wanting a small nudge to needing a push. — [Bugnet, "How to design a hint system for puzzles"](https://bugnet.io/blog/how-to-design-a-hint-system-for-puzzles). Detect stuckness with time- or behaviour-based triggers and offer 3-5 levels from a vague nudge to specific direction. — [Adventure Game Designer Handbook FAQ](https://brycecovert.github.io/adventure-game-designer-handbook/docs/faq.html). Moncage gives each puzzle one to three hint texts meant to nudge rather than spoil. — [Moncage devlog](https://zd2283.itch.io/moncage/devlog/285360/moncage-101-introduction-to-the-hint-systems). Professor Layton charges a hint coin per hint, with three progressively stronger hints per puzzle. — [TV Tropes, Hint System](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/HintSystem) (secondary)
- An adaptive-hint research paper (University of Washington) reports that 68.3% of surveyed users had used an in-game hint system when stuck. — [Hao et al. 2022, UW](https://faculty.washington.edu/weicaics/paper/papers/HaoHZC2022.pdf) (from search excerpt; methodology not reviewed)
- **Escape-room practice (group puzzle play):** "hints are surrender; clues are the game." Hint triggers can be player-requested (never undercuts a team that's about to solve it, but proud teams stay stuck), at the gamemaster's discretion (best when attentive), time-released (fair, but drags slow teams along or leaves fast teams waiting), or automated through checkpoints. Diagnostic: a puzzle needing a hint in about half of games or more needs refinement, and one that almost always needs a hint is "broken." Reader suggestions: ask the team if they want a hint rather than pushing one; don't hard-cap hints. — [David Spira, "An Exploration of Escape Room Hint Systems," Room Escape Artist (2019)](https://roomescapeartist.com/2019/10/24/escape-room-hint-systems/)
- **Super Guide (Nintendo):** in New Super Mario Bros. Wii, a Super Guide block appears after repeated failure (sources say 8 deaths), offering a prerecorded developer playthrough and the option to provisionally clear the course and skip ahead. In NSMB U it appears after 5 failures. — [Super Mario Wiki, Super Guide](https://www.mariowiki.com/Super_Guide); [Iwata Asks: NSMB Wii](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/nsmb/0/5). Miyamoto said: "I feel guilty when the Super Guide block appears... I feel inspired to finish the level on my own." The prompt motivates as well as rescues. — [Vooks, "Miyamoto reflects on the Super Guide"](https://vooks.net/miyamoto-reflects-on-the-super-guide)
- The 2025 Super Mario Galaxy 1+2 release adds a new assist mode. — [Nintendo Wire (2025)](https://nintendowire.com/news/2025/09/18/super-mario-galaxy-12-adds-a-new-assist-mode-bonus-storybook-chapters-and-amiibo-support/) (headline only; details not verified)
- **Failure design:** "Live and learn": players should be able to finish without dying if careful, and "danger should be survivable if the player is clever." "Real time is bad drama": give slack in timed puzzles. — [Gilbert 1989](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck). Avoid death spirals and make failures feel like the player's own fault so they can see what went wrong. — [Game Developer, KTANE talk](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/finding-the-fun-in-bomb-defusal-with-i-keep-talking-and-nobody-explodes-i-)
- **Skill-gap helpers:** Unravel Two's combine mechanic lets one player carry the other through hard platforming. — [GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/you-and-me-till-the-end-unravel-two-review/). Galaxy's Co-Star mode lets a parent assist a child. — [Iwata Asks](https://www.nintendo.co.uk/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-Super-Mario-Galaxy/Volume-4-Shigeru-Miyamoto/1-The-Old-Issue-of-Two-Player-Play/1-The-Old-Issue-of-Two-Player-Play-220647.html)
- Valve watched players silently and adjusted gameplay "to what players look like they need," asking them to retell the story at the end to check comprehension. — [Game Developer, Portal GDC](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success)

### Inferences
- **Tiered, in-fiction hints for two players.** Make the hint book a "Grandma's margin notes on the map" (or similar). Tier 1 restates the goal and the pair's abilities ("Someone small might fit somewhere..."). Tier 2 points at the place ("Look near the fireplace"). Tier 3 names the action for each sibling. Tier 4 is "show me," a short ghost demo. Either player can open it, but escalating past tier 2 should need *both* players to agree (a two-button "we want a bigger hint"), so one impatient player can't spoil it for the other.
- **Split hints for split information:** for information puzzles, deliver the hint to the player who *lacks* the insight, which pushes them to ask their partner the right question.
- **Proactive nudges, not pushes:** after N minutes without progress on a node (from the dependency chart), the map glows or a sibling remarks on it ("Hmm, that vent looks big enough for me..."), using the vocalization pattern. Gilbert's "reward intent" and Oger's leftover-dialogue redirects are the models.
- **Soft fail only:** no lives and no game over. Falls respawn the sibling next to the partner in about 1-2 s, and puzzle elements reset locally, not the whole room. Timed sequences secretly wait for the slower player.
- **Checkpoints at every dependency-chart node** in the browser state. Reconnects (which happen often in online play) must restore both players at the last solved node.
- **Skip as a last resort:** after a high threshold of failure or time, both players see an optional "let the map show the way" (Super Guide analogue). Keep it optional and slightly reluctant (Miyamoto's "guilt" framing motivates players to try again), and never make it mandatory.
- **Accessibility for kids and non-gamers:** minimal reading (icons plus voiced or illustrated clues), forgiving jump timing (coyote time, generous ledge grabs), no fast reflex requirements on the critical path (KTANE's "simple and deliberate inputs"), colour-blind-safe clue palettes, and pairing every colour clue with a shape or animal.
- **Instrument hint usage per puzzle.** Per the escape-room rule, any puzzle where 50% or more of pairs open tier 2 or higher in playtests needs redesign.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve details of Thimbleweed Park's in-game hint line (PC Gamer article body truncated).
- No research found specifically on hint systems for *co-op* pairs, such as who controls escalation or whether hints should be shared. The two-player consent idea above is my own.
- No sourced data on optimal stuck-time thresholds before a nudge for children.

## 6. Randomised or procedural puzzle answers (replay value, stopping answer look-ups)

### Takeaway
The proven pattern is to keep the *reasoning* fixed and randomise the *values*: codes, which symbol maps to which, which direction counts as north. Myst (2020/21) does this as an opt-in mode and Silent Hill uses random codes. Keep Talking goes further, procedurally generating each bomb against a fixed rulebook. This defeats walkthroughs and keeps split-information puzzles honest, because the partner's information is the only way to the answer.

### Cited Findings
- **Keep Talking and Nobody Explodes:** "Each level will generate the bomb and its modules to be defused in a procedural manner." Rules were originally generated procedurally so they could be shuffled between trade-show demos. The shipped game uses static rules, with a framework that could mix them up in future versions. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keep_Talking_and_Nobody_Explodes)
- **Myst (2020/2021 remake):** at the start the player chooses original or randomised solutions. The randomizer is off by default and aimed at veterans. — [Wccftech review](https://wccftech.com/review/myst-remake-a-fleeting-memory/). Walkthroughs mark randomised puzzles and note that "how you get to the solution will be the same." — [ProGameGuides, Myst Remake walkthrough](https://progameguides.com/general-walkthroughs/myst-remake-walkthrough). Examples include a randomised code for a sound-puzzle door in the Selenitic Age and a randomised "north" for tuning sound receivers. — [Reddit r/myst thread (mirror)](https://redlib.hbubli.cc/r/myst/comments/iw269a/all_possible_puzzle_randomization_in_remake_of). Some puzzles are fully randomised with every combination possible, while others draw from only a small set of values. — [Speedrun.com Myst (2020) guide](https://www.speedrun.com/myst_2020/guides/gv00f)
- **Silent Hill 2:** puzzle codes "are random every time." — [Silent Hill Memories, SH2 puzzles](https://www.silenthillmemories.net/sh2/puzzles_en.htm)
- **Possibility space as anti-brute-force:** larger state spaces make puzzles harder and block brute-forcing ("a double-edged sword"). The minimum number of steps from start to solution is a useful difficulty metric. — [Game Developer, explorable explanation](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-make-a-good-puzzle---an-explorable-explanation)
- **Unverified premises (checked and not supported):** I found no evidence that Outer Wilds randomises its Eye coordinates per save. Players discuss looking them up, and randomisers exist only as mods. — [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/753640/discussions/2/2568689796942679457); [Outer Wilds Randomizer mod](https://outerwildsmods.com/mods/randomizer/). I found no source confirming randomised solutions in We Were Here Together, or a randomizer in the 2024 Riven remake.

### Inferences
- **Randomise values, never logic.** For each split-information puzzle, define a fixed reasoning template ("the brother sees the order of animals on the nursery frieze; the sister must set the lock dials to match") and draw values per session from a seed shared by both clients. Look-ups become pointless, and replays (siblings, a parent replaying with a second child) stay fresh.
- **Generate from curated sets, not pure randomness.** Like Myst's "small subset" puzzles, choose from hand-checked, kid-describable symbol sets (animals, colours, fruit) and filter out ambiguous or look-alike combinations, for example never two similar birds together. KTANE's O/0 lesson applies.
- **Seed per chapter and save the seed** so reconnects and checkpoint restores keep the same answers.
- **Keep physical/ability puzzles hand-authored.** Randomise only the information layer (codes, orders, which of three vents is open), so the level geometry and the kishōtenketsu teaching order stay intact.
- A randomised answer also enforces co-op: if the answer exists only on the partner's screen this session, one player can't solve it from memory or a guide.

### Gaps
- No designer talk or postmortem found on *how* Myst's randomizer was designed or tested. The evidence comes from reviews and community guides.
- No sources found on procedural answer generation specifically for children's or co-op puzzle games.

## 7. Common mistakes: moon logic, pixel hunting, unclear affordances, too much text, brute-forceable puzzles

### Takeaway
Most failures come from breaking the player's ability to *reason*: solutions without in-world logic (moon logic), hidden or unreadable interactables (pixel hunting, unclear affordances), noise and text that use up working memory, and puzzles that can be solved by guessing or by one player alone. The remedies are well documented: logical cause and effect, visible and honest presentation, minimal elements, watching playtesters silently, and per-puzzle hint-rate tracking.

### Cited Findings
- **Moon logic, the canonical example:** Gabriel Knight 3's (1999) cat-hair-moustache puzzle, where the player disguises Gabriel with cat hair to get a man's rental motorcycle. Erik Wolpaw's Old Man Murray article "Death of Adventure Games" (Sept 2000) used it to argue adventure games were killing themselves with ridiculous puzzles. The puzzle reportedly replaced a cut design for budget reasons, and the article's broader claim is disputed. — [Wikipedia, Cat hair mustache puzzle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cat_hair_mustache_puzzle); [AV Club, "The cat mustache was never the issue here"](https://www.avclub.com/the-death-of-adventure-games-the-cat-mustache-was-never-the-issue-here)
- Gilbert's remedies for moon logic: *Arbitrary puzzles*: "Puzzles and their solutions need to make sense," and trial and error or luck means the puzzle is bad. *Unconnected events*: there must be a reason an action affects the target. *Backwards puzzles*: show the problem before the solution. — [Gilbert 1989](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)
- **Guessing:** "If players are forced to guess, or feel forced to guess, they're either going to be wrong and feel cheated, or be right and feel unsatisfied." — Ben Kane, Steel Crate. [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/finding-the-fun-in-bomb-defusal-with-i-keep-talking-and-nobody-explodes-i-)
- **Pixel hunting / hidden information:** "a good puzzle wants to be solved." Choose the view that shows the whole puzzle and don't obscure pieces. — [Game Developer, explorable explanation](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-make-a-good-puzzle---an-explorable-explanation). Reward intent: consider the character's proximity to objects and give the player "the benefit of the doubt." — [Gilbert 1989](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck). Offer several valid items (four things work as a mop head) so finding one is likely. — [Oger](https://jboger.substack.com/p/monkeyisland)
- **Unclear affordances:** art should set expectations of behaviour. — [Game Developer, explorable explanation](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-make-a-good-puzzle---an-explorable-explanation). Portal's confusing force field was replaced by glass. — [Game Developer, Portal GDC](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success). A capital O that looked like a zero confused players reading serial numbers aloud, so it was removed. — [Game Developer, KTANE](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/finding-the-fun-in-bomb-defusal-with-i-keep-talking-and-nobody-explodes-i-). Puzzles should be recognisable as puzzles on sight. — [Game Developer, Blow IndieCade](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/indiecade-inside-jonathan-blow-s-puzzle-design-process)
- **Too much text / tutorials:** teach "not with a series of terrible tutorials, but gameplay." — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2015/03/17/super-mario-3d-world-design/). Baba Is You has no tutorial and teaches through trivially simple opening puzzles. — [Hamatti notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/Gaming/Puzzle-game-design). Valve was "ruthless about trimming the fat" so story never intruded on gameplay. — [Game Developer, Portal GDC](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success). Oger contrasts RtMI's two concrete objectives with games that bury players in mission briefings. — [Oger](https://jboger.substack.com/p/monkeyisland)
- **Noise and red herrings:** noise uses working memory without helping, and red herrings are noise. — [Brett Taylor via Hamatti notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/Gaming/Puzzle-game-design). Prefer objects that seem useless but later get used in "completely new and unexpected ways" over pure red herrings. — [Game Developer, explorable explanation](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-make-a-good-puzzle---an-explorable-explanation)
- **Execution walls:** "Knowing the solution but not being able to execute it sucks." — [Brett Taylor via Hamatti notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/Gaming/Puzzle-game-design). Use simple, deliberate inputs. — [Game Developer, KTANE](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/finding-the-fun-in-bomb-defusal-with-i-keep-talking-and-nobody-explodes-i-)
- **One-player-solvable co-op:** a co-op puzzle one person can solve doesn't pass. — [AWN, Wolpaw](https://www.awn.com/print/blog/talking-portal-2-valve-software-s-erik-wolpaw). Unravel Two was criticised because about 70% of puzzles were solvable with one Yarny. — [Destructoid review (via search)](https://vip-develop.destructoid.com/?p=224565)
- **"I forgot to pick it up" and dead ends:** never require an item the player could have missed without a way to get it again. — [Gilbert 1989](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck). Maniac Mansion was "full of dead end puzzles." — [Gilbert 2014](https://grumpygamer.com/puzzle_dependency_charts)
- **Players don't report confusion, so observe:** KTANE's wire-order ambiguity was never reported and was found only by listening. — [Game Developer, KTANE](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/finding-the-fun-in-bomb-defusal-with-i-keep-talking-and-nobody-explodes-i-). "Sit down and watch people play your game." — [Game Developer, Portal GDC](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success). Puzzles that almost always need hints are broken. — [Room Escape Artist](https://roomescapeartist.com/2019/10/24/escape-room-hint-systems/)

### Inferences
- **Pre-flight checklist for each puzzle in this game:**
  1. Can I write its solution sentence, naming who does what?
  2. Is the cause and effect visible in the storybook art? Does a high shelf *look* reachable only by the sister, and does a gap *look* brother-sized?
  3. Is every interactable readable at a glance, for example with a subtle outline or sparkle when a sibling is near?
  4. Can either player solve it alone (character-switching, physics exploits, guessing a 3-digit code)? If so, redesign it.
  5. Does it need reading? If so, can an icon replace the words?
  6. Is the clue describable over pings or symbols, without left/right confusion between two screens?
  7. In playtests, what share of pairs open tier-2 hints? 50% or more means redesign.
- **Brute-force guards for online co-op:** limit dial or code attempts with a soft cooldown and a "maybe ask your partner" nudge rather than a penalty. Use larger randomised value spaces for information puzzles (Section 6). Make physics puzzles need simultaneous presence (two pressure plates, a carry-and-hold).
- **Storybook art helps affordances:** a consistent visual language (for example, warm-gold edges on things the sister can grab high, a soft blue glow on gaps the brother can enter) teaches the split abilities without text, the way Braid's green glow marked rewind-immune objects.
- **Physics-based moon logic is still moon logic.** "The sock drawer opens the attic" has to have a visible reason, such as a string tied between them.
